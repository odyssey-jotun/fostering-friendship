The Research

Social inclusion aims to create fair and equitable opportunities for everyone. The consequence of it enables underserved and neglected groups to participate in various activities, such as sports and recreation. Our mission aims to understand, produce, and develop research based practices that enhance quality of life by fostering community acceptance, belonging, and trust, especially with at risk foster kids. This is why we based all of our approaches from peer reviewed research with this precious demographic.

Study 01

The Role of Recreation in Promoting Social Inclusion

Donnelly, P., & Coakley, J. (2002). The Role of Recreation in Promoting Social Inclusion. Ottawa, ON: Laidlaw Foundation. Accessed 28 Mar. 2024

In “The Role of Recreation in Promoting Social Inclusion,” Peter Donnelly and Jay Coakley explore how recreational activities can enhance social inclusion. They argue that recreation creates opportunities for individuals from various backgrounds to connect, build relationships, and foster a sense of community. The authors highlight the numerous benefits of recreational participation, such as improved self-esteem, mental and physical health, and increased social cohesion, particularly for marginalized groups. They also discuss the barriers to access, including economic disparities, social stigma, and inadequate infrastructure, which can limit the inclusivity of recreational activities. The report stresses the need for inclusive recreational policies and programs that address these challenges and meet the diverse needs of community members. By prioritizing inclusivity and equity, Donnelly and Coakley believe recreation can play a significant role in promoting social inclusion and enhancing community well-being.

Direct Quotes:

  • “Recreational activities provide a unique platform for individuals from diverse backgrounds to interact, build relationships, and develop a sense of community.”
  • “Participation in recreational activities can significantly enhance self-esteem, mental and physical health, and social cohesion, particularly for marginalized groups.”
  • “Barriers such as economic disparities, social stigma, and inadequate infrastructure can hinder the inclusive potential of recreational activities.”
  • “Inclusive recreational policies and programs that prioritize equity are essential to maximize the social benefits of recreation and promote community well-being.”

Study 02

The Role of Organized Sport in the Education and Health of American Youth

Ewing, M., Seefeldt, V., & Brown, T. (1996). The Role of Organized Sport in the Education and Health of American Youth. In The Role of Sports in Youth Development: The Carnegie Meeting Papers, edited by A. Poinsett. Accessed 23 June 2024

Ewing, Seefeldt, and Brown’s 1996 work, “The Role of Organized Sport in the Education and Health of American Youth,” explores the multifaceted benefits of youth participation in organized sports. The authors highlight how organized sports contribute to physical health, promoting regular exercise and reducing the risk of obesity. Additionally, they emphasize the educational benefits, noting that sports can improve academic performance through enhanced discipline, time management, and teamwork skills. The paper also discusses the social advantages, such as building friendships and developing social skills. Furthermore, the authors point out the potential for organized sports to instill important life values like perseverance, leadership, and fair play. Overall, the work underscores organized sports’ critical role in the holistic development of young people.

Direct Quotes:

  • “Evidence shows that young people’s involvement in sports produces multiple benefits. At their best, sports programs promote responsible social behaviors and tremendous academic success, confidence in one’s physical abilities, an appreciation of personal health and fitness, and strong social bonds with individuals and institutions. Teachers attribute these results to the discipline and work ethic that sports require.”
  • Regarding the benefits of sports participation for youth development, Ewing stressed that children are not miniature adults. Though able to mimic adult skills, they need to readily understand many sports concepts, such as fair play. “‘If I’m doing it, it’s fair,’” she quoted young people’s thinking. “‘If I have to take a turn and let somebody else do it, that’s not fair.’ That’s the level of their understanding.” She said that sports can provide a venue for helping young people work toward ethical understanding.
  • “Can good leaders be developed from participation in sports? ‘They can if we teach for leadership,’ Ewing responded. ‘That means you have to give kids a chance to take responsibility, make some decisions, and be more integrally involved in what happens in their sports program, rather than simply telling them and constantly making all decisions and at times absolving them from taking responsibility.’”

Study 03

Sport and Social Inclusion

Frisby, W., & Ponic, P. (2013). Sport and Social Inclusion. In Sport Policy in Canada, edited by Lucie Thibault and Jean Harvey, University of Ottawa Press, pp. 381–403. Accessed 28 Mar. 2024

In their chapter “Sport and Social Inclusion,” Wendy Frisby and Pamela Ponic discuss how sport can be a powerful tool for promoting social inclusion in Canada. They examine various ways in which sports participation can break down social barriers and foster a sense of belonging among marginalized groups, such as women, ethnic minorities, and individuals with disabilities. The authors argue that inclusive sports policies can enhance social cohesion and improve community well-being. They also address the challenges and limitations of using sport for social inclusion, noting that structural inequalities and discriminatory practices can sometimes undermine these efforts. The chapter emphasizes the need for intentional, well-designed sports programs and policies prioritizing inclusivity and equity. Overall, Frisby and Ponic advocate for the strategic use of sport to create more inclusive and equitable communities in Canada.

Direct Quotes:

  • “We define social inclusion as creating just and equitable systems that facilitate people’s choices and opportunities to engage (or not) in a wide range of social and democratic activities, including sport and recreation.”
  • “The well-intentioned goal of social inclusion is to create improved quality of life for all persons, regardless of their situations and positions in society.”
  • “Since social inclusion is complex, it can mean different things to different people. Ponic and Frisby (2010) found that it had multiple meanings for women living in poverty who were involved in a health promotion project designed to increase their participation in community recreation. The women reported that feelings of acceptance, recognition, belonging, safety, and trust were central to their social inclusion in community activities. They also pointed to the importance of relationships based on respect and support, and the crucial role that community organizations with caring staff can play in facilitating their involvement. The women confirmed that a citizen engagement or community development approach that facilitated their input into policy and program development supported their feelings of social inclusion and resulted in activities that better met their needs and interests.”

Study 04

Unpacking Assumptions about 'Inclusion' in Community-Based Health Promotion: Perspectives of Women Living in Poverty

Ponic, P., & Frisby, W. (2010). Unpacking Assumptions about ‘Inclusion’ in Community-Based Health Promotion: Perspectives of Women Living in Poverty. Qualitative Health Research, 11, 1519–1531. Accessed 23 June 2024

In “Unpacking Assumptions about ‘Inclusion’ in Community-Based Health Promotion: Perspectives of Women Living in Poverty,” Ponic and Frisby examine the concept of inclusion in health promotion programs from the viewpoint of impoverished women. They identify vital assumptions often undermining genuine inclusion, such as the belief that all community members have equal participation opportunities. The authors argue that these programs frequently overlook the unique barriers faced by women in poverty, including limited access to resources and systemic discrimination. By highlighting these issues, Ponic and Frisby call for more nuanced, equitable approaches to health promotion that genuinely consider the needs and circumstances of marginalized populations. Their research underscores the importance of designing health interventions that are not only accessible but also actively inclusive of the most vulnerable community members.

Direct Quotes:

  • “Inclusion is not just about physical presence; it involves meaningful participation and influence in decision-making processes.”
  • “True inclusion necessitates a redistribution of power and resources to allow marginalized groups to have a genuine voice.”
